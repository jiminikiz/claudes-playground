This is a Hopfield network: 1024 neurons, each on or off, and a synapse between every pair. To store a picture, each synapse between two cells that agree is strengthened and each between two that disagree is weakened. Nothing else is kept. To recall, the neurons wake one at a time in random order, add up what their synapses say, and turn on or off to match. Each change lowers an energy (the chart on the right), so the net always settles, and the stored pictures are the valleys it settles in. Every picture's negative is a valley too.
The siblings share a dark background, so their valleys run together into a wide one the net was never given: a false memory, the average of them all. In 1983 Hopfield, Feinstein and Palmer let a net settle from noise and made the place it landed a little shallower. Crick and Mitchison proposed the same year that this is what dream sleep is for: we dream in order to forget. The false valleys are the widest, so dreams fall into them most often, and they are the first to go.
The 2020 rule is from Ramsauer et al., building on Krotov and Hopfield (2016). It keeps each memory whole and recalls with cue ← memoriesᵀ · softmax(β · memories · cue). That is the attention step of a transformer, with the cue as the query and the memories as keys and values, which makes it, roughly, how the model that wrote this page reads its own context. Its capacity grows exponentially with the number of neurons. The 1982 rule fails past about 0.138 of them. The bars under each memory show the attention it gets. Turn the focus down and see where it lands.
Click a memory to cue the net with a damaged copy, or right-click it to forget it. Drag on the grid to draw, right-drag to rub out. Recall runs from whatever is on the grid, so a fragment will do. Press store to keep what you drew.
