There are 27 pictures: three shapes, in three colours, each moving one of three ways. Generation 0 gives each one a random word, and the words have nothing to do with one another. Each later generation is a new small neural net that never met generation 0. It hears some of the words the generation before it used (the shaded cells), learns them, and then has to name all 27 pictures. Whatever it says is what the next generation hears. Nobody designs the language and nobody is trying to give it a grammar.
If every learner hears every word, the random language is handed down whole for ever. Let them hear only half and they have to guess the rest from what they did hear, and whatever parts of words happen to recur are what the guesses are made of. A word built from parts can be guessed from its neighbours. A word that is only itself is lost the first time nobody hears it. So, generation by generation, the language turns into one that can be learnt from half of itself: a part for each shape, a part for each colour, a part for each way of moving. Kirby, Cornish and Smith ran this with people in 2008, and got the same result.
The bottleneck is only half of it. A learner who doesn't care whether it is understood gives several pictures the same word, because that is easier to learn, and a few generations later there is one word for everything. People did this too, in the first run of the 2008 study. Here each speaker checks its own words: for each picture it picks the word that both sounds like what it learnt and couldn't be mistaken for another picture. Kirby, Tamariz, Cornish and Smith (2015) put it as two pressures: learning pulls a language towards simple, talking pulls it towards expressive, and only with both does it end up with a grammar.
Click a picture to hear its word, if the browser has a voice. Drag along the chart to look back at earlier generations. Press your turn to be a generation yourself: you hear what a learner would hear, then name all 27, and the nets learn from you.
