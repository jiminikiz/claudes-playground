The strip is the last few thousand steps: how much of the dish each kind fills, stacked, with parasites and the white strain in white, and empty water at the top.
pointer:
Drag on the dish to use the pointer. Parasites drops a clump of them. Stir mixes the molecules under it, as a finger would. Dye marks the molecules under it with a new colour that their copies inherit, and switches to the dye view. Dye a spiral's centre and the colour spreads through the whole spiral; dye anywhere else and it is washed out to the rim within about 50 steps. Wipe clears them away: wipe out a spiral and see what happens to the hole.
Every scene is the same rule, on a 300 × 300 dish that wraps round at the edges. A cell holds one molecule or is empty. Each step a molecule falls apart with chance 0.2, and an empty cell may be filled by a copy of one of its four neighbours. Each neighbour stakes a claim of 1, plus 100 if a molecule of the kind before it in the cycle sits beside the line along which it would copy. Against those claims, the cell has a claim of 11 to stay empty. Then the water moves: twice a step, every 2 × 2 block of cells turns a quarter turn one way or the other. Nothing in the rule knows what a spiral is.
Why it matters: before cells, life may have been molecules that copy themselves, like RNA. Eigen and Schuster (1979) showed a lone copier makes too many mistakes to hold much information, and proposed a ring of them, each speeding the copying of the next: a hypercycle. Maynard Smith (1979) objected that a parasite taking help without giving any would kill it. Boerlijst and Hogeweg (1991) answered by putting the ring on a surface instead of in a stirred pot.
